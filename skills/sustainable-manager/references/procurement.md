## Overview

**ISO 20400:2017** provides guidance for integrating sustainability into procurement processes. It applies to all organizations regardless of size, sector, or ownership (public or private).

**Key characteristics:**
- It is a **guidance standard**, not certifiable (unlike ISO 14001)
- Derived from ISO 26000 (Social Responsibility) principles
- Covers environmental, social, and economic dimensions of procurement
- Emphasizes **lifecycle perspective** — total cost and impact, not just purchase price
- Requires **leadership commitment** and cross-functional collaboration

**Why it matters for sustainability reporting:**
- ESRS S2 (Workers in the Value Chain) requires due diligence on supply chain conditions
- ESRS E1 (Climate Change) Scope 3 emissions are heavily driven by purchased goods and services
- ESRS G1 (Business Conduct) covers payment practices and ethical supplier relationships
- ISO 20400 provides the operational framework to deliver on these disclosure requirements

## Seven Core Principles

Derived from ISO 26000, these principles guide all procurement decisions:

### 1. Organizational Governance
- Clear policies, procedures, and accountability for sustainable procurement
- Board/management oversight and commitment
- Integration into corporate strategy, not a parallel process

### 2. Human Rights
- Fair labor practices throughout the supply chain
- No child labor, forced labor, or discrimination
- Freedom of association and collective bargaining
- Due diligence aligned with UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs)

### 3. Labor Practices
- Safe and healthy working conditions at suppliers
- Fair wages and working hours
- Skills development and training opportunities
- Social protection for workers

### 4. Environment
- Minimize environmental impacts across the supply chain
- Pollution prevention, resource efficiency, climate action
- Lifecycle thinking: consider extraction, production, use, and disposal
- Preference for certified and eco-labeled products/services

### 5. Fair Operating Practices
- Anti-corruption and ethical behavior in procurement
- Fair competition and transparent tendering
- Respect for intellectual property
- Responsible political involvement

### 6. Consumer Issues
- Product safety and quality throughout the supply chain
- Honest marketing and information
- Data protection and privacy
- Access and inclusivity

### 7. Community Involvement and Development
- Local sourcing where possible and appropriate
- Support for local economic development
- Technology transfer and capacity building
- Engagement with community stakeholders

## The Procurement Process

ISO 20400 defines 5 phases where sustainability must be integrated:

### Phase 1: Planning
- Conduct sustainability risk assessment for each procurement category
- Identify material sustainability issues (environmental, social, economic)
- Define sustainability requirements and criteria
- Consider alternatives (reduce need, reuse, share, lease vs. buy)
- Apply lifecycle thinking from the outset

**Key questions:**
- What are the main sustainability risks in this category?
- Can the need be met with lower environmental/social impact?
- What lifecycle costs should be considered beyond purchase price?

### Phase 2: Sourcing
- Include sustainability criteria in tender specifications
- Weight sustainability alongside price, quality, and delivery
- Require evidence of sustainability performance (certifications, data, policies)
- Consider pre-qualification based on sustainability minimums

**Key questions:**
- Do specifications include environmental and social requirements?
- Are sustainability criteria weighted in the evaluation matrix?
- Are we asking for evidence, not just declarations?

### Phase 3: Evaluation & Award
- Apply **lifecycle costing** (LCC) — not just upfront price
- Evaluate supplier sustainability performance against defined criteria
- Score environmental management, labor practices, human rights due diligence
- Consider third-party certifications (ISO 14001, SA8000, FSC, MSC, etc.)
- Check for red flags: sanctions, controversies, adverse media

**Key questions:**
- Are we comparing total lifecycle cost, or just unit price?
- Has the supplier demonstrated environmental and social due diligence?
- Are there verified certifications or only self-declarations?

### Phase 4: Contract Management
- Embed sustainability KPIs and commitments in contracts
- Define monitoring and reporting requirements
- Include improvement targets and escalation procedures
- Regular sustainability performance reviews
- Right to audit on sustainability matters

**Key questions:**
- Do contracts include measurable sustainability clauses?
- How will we monitor and verify supplier compliance?
- What happens if sustainability targets are not met?

### Phase 5: Supplier Development
- Support suppliers in improving sustainability capabilities
- Provide training, resources, and technical assistance
- Collaborate on innovation for sustainable solutions
- Build long-term partnerships rather than transactional relationships
- Share best practices across the supply base

**Key questions:**
- Are we investing in supplier capability, not just demanding compliance?
- Do we collaborate on sustainability improvement, or only audit?
- Are we recognizing and rewarding sustainability leaders in our supply chain?

## Supplier Assessment Criteria

A structured framework for evaluating supplier sustainability:

### Environmental Criteria
- Environmental management system (ISO 14001 or equivalent)
- GHG emissions data and reduction targets (Scope 1, 2, and relevant Scope 3)
- Energy efficiency and renewable energy use
- Water management (especially in water-stressed regions)
- Waste management and circular economy practices
- Hazardous substance management (REACH, RoHS compliance)
- Biodiversity and land use impacts
- Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) or LCA data

### Social Criteria
- Labor standards compliance (ILO core conventions)
- Health and safety management (ISO 45001 or equivalent)
- Fair wages (living wage, not just minimum wage)
- Working hours compliance
- No child labor or forced labor
- Freedom of association
- Diversity and inclusion policies
- Community engagement

### Governance Criteria
- Anti-corruption policies and training
- Code of conduct for employees and suppliers
- Whistleblowing mechanisms
- Tax transparency
- Data protection and cybersecurity
- Conflict of interest management

### Economic / Lifecycle Criteria
- Total cost of ownership (not just purchase price)
- Product durability and repairability
- Availability of spare parts
- End-of-life recyclability or take-back programs
- Local economic contribution

## Practical Implementation

### Getting Started Checklist

1. **Assess current state**: Map existing procurement policies and practices against ISO 20400 principles
2. **Identify priority categories**: Focus on high-spend, high-risk procurement categories first
3. **Define minimum requirements**: Set baseline sustainability criteria for all suppliers
4. **Build a supplier code of conduct**: Based on the 7 principles, tailored to your sector
5. **Integrate into processes**: Embed sustainability criteria into existing procurement workflows (don't create parallel systems)
6. **Train procurement teams**: Ensure buyers understand sustainability criteria and how to evaluate them
7. **Monitor and improve**: Establish KPIs, track progress, and iterate

### Maturity Levels

| Level | Description | Characteristics |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| **1 — Ad hoc** | No systematic approach | Sustainability considered only when required by regulation or customer demand |
| **2 — Developing** | Policy exists but limited implementation | Sustainability policy in place, some criteria in tenders, limited monitoring |
| **3 — Practicing** | Systematic integration | Sustainability criteria in all major tenders, supplier assessments, regular reporting |
| **4 — Leading** | Strategic and innovative | Lifecycle costing standard practice, supplier development programs, collaborative innovation, value chain transparency |

### Common Pitfalls
- **Checkbox approach**: Asking for certifications without verifying real performance
- **One-size-fits-all**: Applying the same criteria to all categories regardless of risk
- **Price-only comparison**: Not using lifecycle costing in evaluation
- **Audit fatigue**: Over-auditing without investing in supplier development
- **Scope 3 blind spot**: Not connecting procurement decisions to Scope 3 emissions reporting
