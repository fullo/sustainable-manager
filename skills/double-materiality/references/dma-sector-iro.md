# Pre-mapped IROs by Sector

Reference file for the Double Materiality Assessment skill. Contains sector-specific Impacts, Risks, and Opportunities for each ESRS topical standard across five key sectors.

Use this as a starting point. Always adapt to the user's specific company context, value chain, and geographic scope.

Typical materiality ratings (HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW) indicate expected relevance for the sector based on industry benchmarks, SASB, and GRI sector guidance. These are starting indications, not prescriptive conclusions.

---

## 1. Manufacturing (General Industrial, Ceramics, Metals, Machinery)

### E1 Climate Change — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Direct GHG emissions from combustion processes (kilns, furnaces, boilers) and purchased electricity. In the Italian ceramics district (Sassuolo), natural gas consumption for firing kilns is the dominant emission source, often exceeding 50% of total corporate emissions.
- **Risk**: Carbon pricing exposure through EU ETS and potential CBAM surcharges on imported raw materials (e.g., clays from Turkey, alumina from China). Rising energy costs driven by fossil fuel dependency, with natural gas price volatility creating margin uncertainty.
- **Opportunity**: Energy efficiency investments in kiln heat recovery systems and cogeneration plants, yielding payback periods of 3-5 years. Electrification of low-temperature processes and on-site solar PV installations to reduce Scope 2 and hedge against grid price volatility.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E2 Pollution — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5) and NOx emissions from high-temperature processes affecting air quality in surrounding communities. In densely industrialized districts, cumulative pollution from multiple plants creates regional air quality challenges.
- **Risk**: Tightening EU Industrial Emissions Directive (IED) limits requiring significant air treatment investments (SCR/SNCR systems, advanced filtration). Potential production restrictions during high-pollution episodes under regional environmental ordinances.
- **Opportunity**: Investment in best-available-techniques (BAT) pollution control yielding regulatory compliance headroom and social license to operate. Marketing advantage from certified low-emission production processes.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E3 Water and Marine Resources — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Process water consumption for cooling, washing, and surface treatment. Wastewater discharge containing suspended solids, heavy metals (in metal finishing), or chemical residues.
- **Risk**: Water scarcity risk in Mediterranean industrial basins during summer months, potentially constraining production during drought periods. Regulatory tightening on industrial wastewater discharge limits.
- **Opportunity**: Closed-loop water recycling systems reducing freshwater intake by 60-80%. Rainwater harvesting for non-process uses.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E4 Biodiversity and Ecosystems — Typical materiality: LOW

- **Impact**: Land use for facilities and quarrying of raw materials (clay, sand, stone). Noise and light pollution from 24/7 production operations affecting local fauna.
- **Risk**: Permitting delays if new facilities or expansions overlap with Natura 2000 sites or protected habitats. Quarry rehabilitation obligations at end-of-life.
- **Opportunity**: Ecological restoration of exhausted quarry sites as biodiversity reserves. Green infrastructure on factory premises (green roofs, permeable surfaces).
- **Typical materiality**: LOW

### E5 Circular Economy — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Waste generation from production rejects, packaging materials, and end-of-life equipment. Use of virgin raw materials where recycled alternatives exist.
- **Risk**: Rising raw material costs driven by resource scarcity and EU Critical Raw Materials Act restrictions. Extended producer responsibility (EPR) obligations for packaging and product end-of-life.
- **Opportunity**: Industrial symbiosis within manufacturing districts — one company's waste as another's raw material. Recycled content in products commanding premium positioning in green procurement tenders.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S1 Own Workforce — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Occupational health and safety risks from high-temperature processes, heavy machinery, noise exposure, and repetitive manual tasks. Shift work affecting work-life balance and long-term health.
- **Risk**: Skilled labor shortage in technical manufacturing roles, particularly among younger generations. Rising workplace injury insurance costs (INAIL premiums in Italy). Aging workforce in traditional manufacturing districts.
- **Opportunity**: Investment in automation reducing hazardous manual tasks while upskilling workforce. Welfare programs and academy partnerships improving employer attractiveness and retention.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S2 Workers in the Value Chain — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Working conditions at raw material extraction sites in non-EU countries (mining of minerals, quarrying). Labor conditions of logistics subcontractors and temporary staffing agencies.
- **Risk**: Supply chain due diligence requirements under the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CS3D). Reputational risk from supplier labor violations in lower-cost sourcing countries.
- **Opportunity**: Supplier qualification programs strengthening supply chain resilience and enabling preferential procurement terms with responsible sourcing commitments.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S3 Affected Communities — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Air and noise pollution affecting residential areas adjacent to industrial zones. Economic dependence of local communities on manufacturing employment (monoculture industrial districts).
- **Risk**: Community opposition to facility expansions or new permits. Municipal ordinances restricting operating hours or transportation routes.
- **Opportunity**: Community engagement programs (factory visits, local employment commitments, sponsorships) strengthening social license to operate and local stakeholder relationships.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S4 Consumers and End-users — Typical materiality: LOW

- **Impact**: Product safety considerations for consumer-facing products (tiles, household appliances, machinery). Chemical content and VOC emissions from finished products during use phase.
- **Risk**: Product liability claims and recall costs. Evolving consumer safety regulation under EU General Product Safety Regulation.
- **Opportunity**: Third-party product safety and sustainability certifications (EPD, Ecolabel) as competitive differentiators in B2B and B2C markets.
- **Typical materiality**: LOW

### G1 Business Conduct — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Governance practices influencing fair competition, anti-corruption, and responsible lobbying. Payment practices affecting SME suppliers in the value chain.
- **Risk**: Anti-corruption enforcement under Italian Decree 231/2001 and EU anti-bribery framework. Antitrust risk in concentrated industrial districts with potential coordination on pricing.
- **Opportunity**: Robust compliance programs (Model 231, whistleblowing systems) reducing legal risk and improving eligibility for public procurement and EU funding programs.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

## 2. Energy and Utilities

### E1 Climate Change — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Direct GHG emissions from fossil fuel combustion for power generation (Scope 1). Methane leakage from natural gas distribution networks. Enabling of downstream emissions through energy supplied to customers.
- **Risk**: Stranded asset risk for fossil fuel generation capacity as EU tightens 2030/2040 climate targets. Carbon price escalation under EU ETS Phase IV, directly impacting cost of generation. Physical climate risks (heatwaves reducing thermal plant efficiency, droughts reducing hydroelectric output).
- **Opportunity**: Renewable energy capacity expansion capturing green premium in PPAs and government incentives. Grid-scale battery storage enabling dispatchable renewable supply. Green hydrogen production leveraging existing infrastructure.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E2 Pollution — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: SO2, NOx, and particulate emissions from thermal power plants. Coal ash and flue gas desulfurization waste requiring managed disposal. Thermal pollution of waterways from cooling water discharge.
- **Risk**: Mercury and heavy metal emission limits under IED BAT conclusions for large combustion plants. Contaminated site remediation liabilities for decommissioned coal and oil plants.
- **Opportunity**: Transition to natural gas and renewable generation eliminating air pollution emissions. Remediation of legacy sites unlocking land value in urban areas.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E3 Water and Marine Resources — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Massive cooling water abstraction from rivers and coastal areas for thermal power generation. Potential thermal shock to aquatic ecosystems from heated water discharge. Water consumption for hydroelectric reservoir management.
- **Risk**: Water availability constraints during droughts directly affecting hydroelectric and thermal generation capacity. Regulatory tightening on thermal discharge limits and abstraction permits.
- **Opportunity**: Dry cooling and closed-loop cooling systems reducing water dependency. Floating solar PV on reservoirs increasing dual-use of water infrastructure.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E4 Biodiversity and Ecosystems — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Habitat disruption from hydroelectric dam construction and wind farm siting. Bird and bat mortality from wind turbines. Submarine cable impacts on marine habitats for offshore installations.
- **Risk**: Permitting delays and project cancellations due to environmental impact assessment (EIA) findings. Legal challenges from environmental NGOs blocking infrastructure projects.
- **Opportunity**: Biodiversity net gain programs around renewable energy sites. Artificial reef creation around offshore wind foundations enhancing marine biodiversity.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E5 Circular Economy — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: End-of-life management of solar panels, wind turbine blades, and battery storage systems. Coal ash and nuclear waste management. Copper and rare earth demand for grid infrastructure.
- **Risk**: Lack of mature recycling infrastructure for first-generation wind blades and solar panels reaching end-of-life in the 2030s. Critical raw material supply constraints for grid expansion.
- **Opportunity**: Development of blade recycling technologies and circular supply chains for solar panel materials. Second-life applications for grid batteries.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S1 Own Workforce — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: High-risk work environments (high voltage, confined spaces, heights) creating occupational safety hazards. Just transition challenges as fossil fuel workers need reskilling for renewable energy roles.
- **Risk**: Skilled workforce shortage in renewable energy technical roles (grid engineers, wind technicians). Aging workforce in traditional utility operations with institutional knowledge loss.
- **Opportunity**: Just transition programs creating competitive advantage in talent attraction. Training academies building pipeline of specialized renewable energy technicians.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S2 Workers in the Value Chain — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Working conditions in solar panel manufacturing supply chains (polysilicon production, cell assembly). Labor practices of subcontractors for construction and maintenance of energy infrastructure.
- **Risk**: Supply chain human rights due diligence requirements under CS3D. Reputational and legal risk from forced labor allegations in polysilicon supply chains.
- **Opportunity**: Qualified contractor management programs ensuring safety and labor standards across the supply chain.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S3 Affected Communities — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Energy poverty affecting vulnerable customers. Visual and noise impact of wind farms and transmission lines on rural communities. Displacement for dam construction.
- **Risk**: Community opposition to renewable energy projects (NIMBY) delaying or blocking permitting. Political pressure for regulated tariff freezes affecting revenue.
- **Opportunity**: Community energy schemes and benefit-sharing models creating local acceptance. Social tariff programs reducing energy poverty while building brand trust.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S4 Consumers and End-users — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Energy affordability for residential and business consumers. Service reliability and power quality affecting vulnerable users (hospitals, elderly). Data privacy for smart meter rollouts.
- **Risk**: Regulatory intervention on pricing during energy crises. Customer switching in liberalized markets. Cybersecurity risks to grid infrastructure affecting service continuity.
- **Opportunity**: Digital energy services (demand response, energy management platforms) creating new revenue streams and customer loyalty.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### G1 Business Conduct — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Market power and pricing practices in concentrated energy markets. Political influence and lobbying on energy policy and subsidies. Tax practices in multi-jurisdictional operations.
- **Risk**: Antitrust investigations into market manipulation or price coordination. Anti-corruption risk in infrastructure procurement and permitting processes. Regulatory sanctions for market abuse.
- **Opportunity**: Transparent governance and stakeholder engagement strengthening regulatory relationships and social license. ESG-linked executive compensation aligning management incentives.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

## 3. Food and Beverage

### E1 Climate Change — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Agricultural supply chain emissions (methane from livestock, N2O from fertilizers, land use change). Processing facility energy consumption for refrigeration, cooking, and packaging. Cold chain logistics emissions across distribution.
- **Risk**: Climate change impacts on agricultural yields (drought, flooding, heat stress on crops and livestock) disrupting supply and increasing input costs. Carbon footprint labeling regulations potentially shifting consumer preferences.
- **Opportunity**: Regenerative agriculture programs in the supply chain sequestering carbon while improving soil health and long-term yield resilience. Plant-based product lines with lower carbon footprint capturing growing market segment.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E2 Pollution — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Pesticide and fertilizer runoff from agricultural supply chain contributing to eutrophication and groundwater contamination. Food processing wastewater with high organic load (BOD/COD). Packaging waste pollution.
- **Risk**: EU Farm to Fork strategy restricting pesticide use by 50% by 2030, requiring supply chain reformulation. PFAS ("forever chemicals") regulation affecting food contact materials and packaging.
- **Opportunity**: Organic and integrated pest management supply chains reducing pollution while commanding price premiums. Advanced wastewater treatment enabling water reuse.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E3 Water and Marine Resources — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Agricultural irrigation as the largest water consumption driver (up to 70% of freshwater use globally). Water-intensive processing operations (washing, cleaning, beverages). Water stress in Mediterranean sourcing regions.
- **Risk**: Water scarcity in key agricultural sourcing areas (southern Italy, Spain, North Africa) threatening supply continuity. Water pricing reforms increasing agricultural input costs. Aquifer depletion in intensive farming regions.
- **Opportunity**: Precision irrigation reducing water consumption by 30-50% in the agricultural supply chain. Water stewardship certifications (AWS) strengthening brand positioning and supply security.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E4 Biodiversity and Ecosystems — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Agricultural land use driving habitat loss and biodiversity decline. Monoculture farming reducing agrobiodiversity. Pollinator decline from pesticide use affecting crop yields systemically.
- **Risk**: EU Nature Restoration Law requiring ecosystem restoration on agricultural land. Deforestation regulation (EUDR) requiring due diligence on commodity supply chains (soy, palm oil, cocoa, coffee, cattle).
- **Opportunity**: Biodiversity-positive sourcing (shade-grown coffee, agroforestry systems) differentiating products. Pollinator corridors on owned agricultural land contributing to ecosystem services.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E5 Circular Economy — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Food waste across the value chain (processing losses, retail shrinkage, consumer waste). Packaging waste from single-use plastics and multi-material laminates. Agricultural by-product utilization.
- **Risk**: EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) mandating recycled content, reuse targets, and design-for-recycling. Food waste reduction targets under Farm to Fork strategy.
- **Opportunity**: Upcycling food processing by-products into animal feed, bioenergy, or functional ingredients. Innovative packaging (compostable, mono-material) capturing sustainability-conscious consumers.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S1 Own Workforce — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Seasonal and temporary employment patterns creating job insecurity. Physically demanding work in processing plants (cold environments, repetitive motions, long shifts). Wage levels in food processing often at or near minimum wage.
- **Risk**: Labor shortage in food processing (post-pandemic structural shift away from manual food industry jobs). High turnover rates increasing training costs and quality risks.
- **Opportunity**: Automation of repetitive and hazardous tasks improving working conditions. Living wage commitments and stable employment contracts improving retention and productivity.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S2 Workers in the Value Chain — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Agricultural labor conditions including seasonal migrant workers in harvesting (documented exploitation in Italian agriculture — "caporalato"). Working conditions in tropical commodity supply chains (cocoa, coffee, palm oil).
- **Risk**: CS3D due diligence requirements for agricultural supply chains. Reputational damage from media exposure of labor exploitation in supply chains. Italian anti-caporalato law (Law 199/2016) creating joint liability for buyer companies.
- **Opportunity**: Fair trade and direct sourcing programs building resilient supply chains and premium brand positioning. Traceability systems (blockchain, satellite monitoring) enabling supply chain transparency.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S3 Affected Communities — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Economic impact on rural communities dependent on agricultural supply chains. Land use competition between food production and urban expansion. Access to healthy and affordable food in underserved communities.
- **Risk**: Community opposition to intensive farming operations (odor, traffic, water use). Local government restrictions on agricultural practices.
- **Opportunity**: Local sourcing programs supporting rural economies and reducing transport emissions. Community nutrition programs strengthening brand trust and social license.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S4 Consumers and End-users — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Nutritional quality and health impact of food products (sugar, salt, ultra-processed ingredients). Food safety and allergen management. Responsible marketing, especially to children.
- **Risk**: EU regulatory pressure on nutritional labeling (Nutri-Score), front-of-pack warnings, and advertising restrictions. Product recall costs and brand damage from food safety incidents.
- **Opportunity**: Healthier product reformulation capturing wellness-oriented consumers. Transparent ingredient sourcing and clean-label products commanding premium pricing.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### G1 Business Conduct — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Fair pricing practices toward agricultural suppliers (buyer power in concentrated food retail). Responsible advertising and marketing claims (greenwashing risk). Tax practices in international operations.
- **Risk**: Unfair trading practices directive enforcement protecting agricultural suppliers. Consumer protection authority scrutiny of "sustainable" and "natural" marketing claims.
- **Opportunity**: Industry leadership in sustainable sourcing standards and supply chain transparency building trust with regulators and consumers.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

## 4. Fashion and Textiles

### E1 Climate Change — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Energy-intensive textile production processes (dyeing, finishing, drying). Supply chain emissions from synthetic fiber production (polyester from petrochemicals) and cotton cultivation. Transport emissions from global supply chains.
- **Risk**: Carbon cost pass-through from energy-intensive suppliers. Consumer and regulatory pressure for carbon footprint disclosure per garment (EU Product Environmental Footprint — PEF). Fast fashion business model scrutiny.
- **Opportunity**: Near-shoring production to Mediterranean basin (Italy, Portugal, Turkey) reducing transport emissions. Renewable energy in production facilities. Low-carbon materials innovation (recycled polyester, organic cotton, innovative fibers).
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E2 Pollution — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Chemical pollution from dyeing and finishing processes — heavy metals (chromium, copper), formaldehyde, PFAS, and other hazardous substances. Microplastic release from synthetic textiles during washing (estimated 500,000 tons per year globally).
- **Risk**: EU REACH regulation updates restricting chemicals used in textile processing. Microplastics regulation requiring filtration systems. ZDHC (Zero Discharge of Hazardous Chemicals) becoming procurement requirement for major brands.
- **Opportunity**: Waterless dyeing technologies (supercritical CO2, digital printing) eliminating chemical wastewater. OEKO-TEX and GOTS certifications as competitive differentiators in the Italian textile excellence market.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E3 Water and Marine Resources — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Extremely water-intensive crop production (conventional cotton requires approximately 10,000 liters per kilogram). Dyeing and finishing processes consuming 50-100 liters per kilogram of fabric. Wastewater discharge with chemical and thermal pollution.
- **Risk**: Water scarcity in key production regions (India, Pakistan for cotton; Mediterranean for finishing). Supply chain disruption from drought-related cotton crop failures.
- **Opportunity**: Closed-loop water systems in dyeing reducing freshwater consumption by 90%. Shift to less water-intensive fibers (hemp, linen, recycled fibers). Water stewardship programs in the supply chain.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E4 Biodiversity and Ecosystems — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Cotton and viscose supply chains driving deforestation and habitat conversion. Pesticide use in conventional cotton farming affecting pollinators and soil health. Leather supply chains linked to deforestation for cattle ranching.
- **Risk**: EUDR applicability to leather and potentially cotton supply chains. Biodiversity due diligence requirements from luxury brands cascading through supply chains.
- **Opportunity**: Certified sustainable raw materials (Better Cotton, FSC-certified viscose) meeting buyer requirements. Regenerative fiber sourcing programs.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E5 Circular Economy — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Post-consumer textile waste — less than 1% of clothing is recycled into new clothing globally. Overproduction and unsold inventory destruction. Blended fiber compositions making recycling technically difficult.
- **Risk**: EU Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles mandating EPR schemes, eco-design requirements, and destruction ban for unsold textiles. Digital Product Passport (DPP) requirement adding cost and complexity.
- **Opportunity**: Circular business models (rental, resale, repair) creating new revenue streams. Mono-material design enabling mechanical recycling. Italian textile recycling expertise (Prato district regenerated wool — "cardato rigenerato") as competitive advantage.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S1 Own Workforce — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Manual labor in garment assembly with repetitive motion injuries, poor ergonomics, and noise exposure. Gender pay gap in fashion industry (predominantly female production workforce, predominantly male management). Creative worker burnout from fast fashion cycle pressure.
- **Risk**: Labor cost increases in traditional production countries driving reshoring decisions. Skills shortage in specialized textile craftsmanship (weaving, finishing) as artisan workforce ages in Italian districts.
- **Opportunity**: Artisan training academies preserving textile heritage while creating employment. Ergonomic automation improving working conditions in Italian production facilities.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S2 Workers in the Value Chain — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Hazardous working conditions in garment factories (structural safety, fire safety, chemical exposure). Low wages below living wage thresholds in major production countries (Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia). Child labor risk in cotton harvesting and informal garment workshops.
- **Risk**: CS3D due diligence obligations for the full textile supply chain. Brand damage from supply chain labor incidents (post-Rana Plaza awareness). Buyer liability for supplier labor violations under emerging legislation.
- **Opportunity**: Transparent supply chain mapping and living wage programs as brand differentiators. Made in Italy positioning leveraging strong domestic labor protections as a quality and ethics signal.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S3 Affected Communities — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Economic dependence of textile districts on fashion industry cycles (Biella for wool, Como for silk, Prato for recycled textiles). Environmental justice concerns in communities near dyeing and finishing facilities.
- **Risk**: District economic decline if production shifts to lower-cost regions. Community health complaints related to industrial pollution.
- **Opportunity**: District-level sustainability initiatives strengthening collective competitiveness. Cultural heritage tourism linked to textile artisanship.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### S4 Consumers and End-users — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Product safety regarding chemical residues in garments (formaldehyde, allergenic dyes, PFAS in waterproof treatments) in contact with skin. Misleading sustainability claims influencing consumer choices.
- **Risk**: EU Green Claims Directive enforcement against unsubstantiated "sustainable" or "eco-friendly" labels. Product safety recalls for non-compliant chemical content.
- **Opportunity**: Verified sustainability labels (EU Ecolabel, GOTS, Cradle to Cradle) driving consumer trust. Digital Product Passport enabling transparency-as-a-service.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### G1 Business Conduct — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Purchasing practices — late payments and last-minute order cancellations pressuring suppliers to cut corners on labor and environment. Intellectual property protection in design-intensive segments.
- **Risk**: Unfair trading practices scrutiny in buyer-supplier relationships. Anti-counterfeiting enforcement costs. Tax optimization through complex multi-jurisdictional structures under increased scrutiny.
- **Opportunity**: Responsible purchasing practices (fair payment terms, no last-minute cancellations) building supplier loyalty and quality consistency.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

## 5. Construction

### E1 Climate Change — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Embodied carbon in building materials — cement production alone accounts for approximately 8% of global CO2 emissions. Energy consumption during construction (diesel machinery, site electricity). Operational energy of delivered buildings over their lifecycle.
- **Risk**: EU Whole Life Carbon (WLC) requirements in the revised Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD). Green public procurement criteria excluding high-carbon materials from tenders. Carbon pricing extending to buildings sector under EU ETS 2.
- **Opportunity**: Low-carbon concrete and alternative binders reducing embodied carbon by 30-50%. Mass timber construction for mid-rise buildings. Nearly Zero Energy Buildings (NZEB) and Passivhaus design expertise as competitive advantage.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E2 Pollution — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Construction site dust, noise, and vibration affecting surrounding communities. Soil contamination from demolition of legacy buildings (asbestos, heavy metals, hydrocarbons). Stormwater runoff from construction sites carrying sediment and pollutants.
- **Risk**: Asbestos remediation liabilities in renovation of pre-1990 buildings (extremely common in Italian building stock). Noise and dust complaints triggering municipal work-stop orders. Contaminated land discovery during excavation requiring expensive remediation.
- **Opportunity**: Advanced dust suppression and noise management technologies enabling urban construction with minimal community impact. Specialization in contaminated site remediation as a growth service line.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### E3 Water and Marine Resources — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Water consumption for concrete production, dust suppression, and site operations. Dewatering activities during foundation construction affecting local water tables. Construction site runoff polluting surface water.
- **Risk**: Water scarcity during summer construction season in southern European regions affecting concrete production schedules. Groundwater contamination liability from construction activities.
- **Opportunity**: Recycled water use in concrete mixing and site operations. Sustainable urban drainage systems (SUDS) as a design service offering.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E4 Biodiversity and Ecosystems — Typical materiality: MEDIUM

- **Impact**: Greenfield land consumption for new construction sealing soil and destroying habitats. Disturbance to protected species during construction (nesting birds, bats in renovation). Tree removal and landscape alteration.
- **Risk**: Biodiversity Net Gain requirements in building permits (following UK model, emerging in EU member states). EIA challenges delaying project timelines and increasing costs.
- **Opportunity**: Green building design integrating biodiversity (green roofs, wildlife corridors, nesting structures). Brownfield redevelopment as preferable to greenfield from biodiversity perspective.
- **Typical materiality**: MEDIUM

### E5 Circular Economy — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Construction and demolition waste (CDW) representing approximately 35% of total waste generated in the EU. Demolition destroying material value embedded in existing structures. Low reuse rates for structural elements and components.
- **Risk**: EU CDW recycling targets (70% by weight — already in force). Minimum recycled content requirements for construction products. Rising landfill taxes and disposal costs for mixed CDW.
- **Opportunity**: Design for Disassembly (DfD) and modular construction enabling future material recovery. Pre-demolition audits and selective demolition maximizing material reuse. Urban mining of existing building stock.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S1 Own Workforce — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Construction has one of the highest occupational fatality and serious injury rates across all sectors. Musculoskeletal disorders from heavy manual labor. Mental health challenges from project-based employment instability and site-to-site mobility.
- **Risk**: Increasing INAIL premium costs from workplace incidents. Criminal liability for site managers under Italian occupational health and safety legislation (D.Lgs. 81/2008). Chronic skilled labor shortage across all construction trades in Europe.
- **Opportunity**: Prefabrication and modular construction moving work from hazardous site conditions to controlled factory environments. Digital tools (BIM, drones) reducing exposure to dangerous inspection tasks. Apprenticeship programs addressing skills gap.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S2 Workers in the Value Chain — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Multi-layered subcontracting chains creating opacity on labor conditions. Undeclared work and labor exploitation in construction subcontracting (documented issue in Italian construction sector). Migrant worker vulnerability to exploitation.
- **Risk**: Joint liability for labor violations by subcontractors under Italian law. CS3D supply chain due diligence extending to construction subcontractors. Fatal accidents in subcontractor operations creating criminal and reputational liability for the general contractor.
- **Opportunity**: Qualified subcontractor management systems (pre-qualification, audits, training) improving safety performance across the project. Digital worker verification systems ensuring legal employment status.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S3 Affected Communities — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Prolonged construction disruption (noise, traffic, dust, access restrictions) affecting quality of life for neighboring residents and businesses. Gentrification effects of urban development displacing existing communities.
- **Risk**: Community opposition blocking or delaying planning permission. Legal challenges from affected residents and businesses seeking compensation for construction impacts. Municipal restrictions on construction hours and methods.
- **Opportunity**: Community engagement from project inception building support and reducing opposition risk. Community benefit agreements (local employment, affordable housing, public space improvements) creating shared value.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### S4 Consumers and End-users — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Building quality and safety affecting occupant health and wellbeing (indoor air quality, thermal comfort, structural safety). Accessibility for persons with disabilities. Energy cost burden for occupants of poorly performing buildings.
- **Risk**: Structural defect liabilities extending 10 years under Italian civil code. Building performance gap (actual energy performance worse than designed) creating warranty and reputational risk. Seismic safety compliance in Italian high-risk zones.
- **Opportunity**: Healthy building certifications (WELL, Fitwel) commanding rental and sale premiums. Energy performance guarantees creating competitive differentiation. Post-occupancy evaluation services.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH

### G1 Business Conduct — Typical materiality: HIGH

- **Impact**: Corruption risk in public procurement and permitting processes. Anti-competitive practices in tender processes (bid rigging). Tax evasion and undeclared revenue in the construction sector.
- **Risk**: Anti-mafia certification requirements (certificazione antimafia) for public works. Anti-corruption enforcement under Decree 231/2001. Exclusion from public tenders for governance violations. ANAC (Italian Anti-Corruption Authority) oversight of public procurement.
- **Opportunity**: Robust compliance and anti-corruption programs (Modello 231) qualifying the company for large public works and EU-funded projects. Transparent governance as differentiator in PPP (public-private partnership) tenders.
- **Typical materiality**: HIGH
